Cognitive load sounds like one of those terms that belongs in a psychology textbook.

A bit stiff. A bit academic. A bit like something someone says in a meeting just before opening a slide with too many arrows on it.

But really, cognitive load is simple.

It is the mental effort someone has to use to understand, process, and complete something. In product design, that matters because every screen asks something from the user. It asks them to read, scan, choose, remember, compare, ignore distractions, make decisions, and take action.

Some of that effort is useful. It helps people learn, understand, and make better decisions.

But some of it is wasted.

It comes from unclear layouts, confusing language, badly prioritised content, unnecessary movement, and screens that make users work harder than they need to.

That is when a simple task starts to feel like a tiring journey.

## When the road gets too busy

I think about cognitive load a lot because it affects me personally.

I have ADHD and dyslexia, so processing information can take more effort for me. If a screen has too much going on, my brain does not always know where to look first. I can find myself sitting there, not doing the task yet, but trying to work out what the task even is.

Sometimes I have to read the same sentence a few times before it lands properly. Sometimes a moving element pulls my attention away from what I was doing. Sometimes I know exactly what I am trying to do, then something distracts me, and the thought just disappears.

It can feel like having a clear thought in your head, then someone right-clicks and deletes it.

That might sound specific to me, but cognitive load is not only a neurodiversity issue. Everyone has a limit. Some people just reach that limit sooner.

People are busy. They are tired. They are working between meetings, filling in forms on their phone, checking something quickly before leaving the house, or trying to complete a task while already carrying a full head of other things.

A product should not assume it has unlimited access to someone's brain.

A good experience should feel like a clear route.

A bad one feels like driving through a town with too many signs, roadworks everywhere, potholes in the left lane, unclear speed limits, and a diversion that sends you past a retail park for no reason.

You might still get there.

But you arrive tired.

## Cognitive load is not just too much stuff

It is easy to think cognitive load just means there is too much on the screen.

Sometimes that is true. A page with too many buttons, alerts, tables, options, and moving elements can quickly become overwhelming. But cognitive load is not only about quantity. It is about whether the user is spending their mental effort on the right things.

Some tasks are naturally complex. Managing patient information, understanding pension choices, reviewing carbon data, or making financial decisions will always require thought. You cannot remove all complexity, and you should not pretend complex things are simple when they are not.

But there is another kind of effort that comes from poor design. This is the roadworks. The confusing signs. The potholes. The unclear labels. The layout that makes people hunt for what they need. The animation that keeps pulling the eye away from the task.

That is the load designers should be trying to reduce.

The goal is not to remove thinking.

The goal is to stop wasting the user's thinking on clutter.

## Products should guide people, not make them hunt

A lot of products are built around the system's structure rather than the user's journey.

The company knows where everything lives. The team knows what each feature means. The product manager knows why a section exists. The developer understands how the data is structured. The designer knows what the page is trying to do.

But the user does not arrive with all that context.

They arrive with a task.

They want to check something, fix something, find something, submit something, understand something, and then move on with their day.

When a product shows everything at once, it is like putting every road sign in the town at the same junction. The information may technically be there, but the user still has to work out which sign matters.

That is where cognitive load builds up. Not always in one big dramatic moment, but in small repeated stops.

A confusing label makes the user pause. A dense table makes them scan harder. A hidden action makes them second-guess themselves. A warning with no clear priority makes them wonder what to deal with first.

Each moment is a little brake tap.

Eventually, the journey feels stop-start.

## Use a Route Check

If a screen feels heavy, the answer is not always to remove things randomly.

A product can look simple and still be hard to use. A clean screen with hidden actions, unclear language, or no obvious next step is not simple. It is just quiet confusion.

So when I look at a screen, I find it useful to think about it like a route.

If the user is trying to get somewhere, what kind of journey are we giving them?

I think of this as a Route Check.

The first question is: what is the destination?

What is the user actually trying to do here? Not what does the business want to show them. Not what features exist in this section. What is the user trying to get done?

If the team cannot answer that clearly, the screen will probably feel unclear too.

The next question is: what is the main road?

Once you know the destination, you need to know the clearest route to get there. The most important action should not be hiding in the same visual weight as everything else. The user should not need to scan the whole map before they can start.

On a dashboard, the main road might be the alert that needs attention. In a table, it might be the two columns that matter most to the decision. In a form, it might be the next field the user needs to complete.

Good design makes the main road visible.

It does not make every road look like a motorway.

Then you can ask: where are the stop signs?

A stop sign could be an unclear label, a vague button, a dense paragraph, a confusing status, a repeated choice, or an alert that appears before the user knows what to do with it.

Some stop signs are needed. If the user is about to make a serious decision, you may need to slow them down.

But many stop signs are accidental. They are bits of friction that have built up over time.

The question is simple: where is the product making the user stop and think when they should be able to keep moving?

## Make the main routes easier to travel

Product teams often want to show all the features because all the features feel important internally.

But not everything is equally important to the user.

Some things are used every day. Some are used once a month. Some are edge cases. Some exist because someone asked for them years ago and nobody has questioned them since.

If everything gets the same visual weight, the user has to do the sorting themselves.

That is tiring.

It is like opening a map where every road is drawn as a motorway. You cannot see the main route, so you have to inspect everything before you can move.

This is where methods like top tasks can help.

Instead of guessing what matters, teams can identify the tasks users are actually trying to complete most often. They can list the tasks they think exist, test those assumptions, and compare them with real user behaviour.

The point is not to ignore everything else. It is to make the most important routes easier to travel.

A product that understands its top tasks feels calmer.

It does not ask the user to scan the whole road network every time they need to get home.

## Search is not a replacement for good routes

Search can be really helpful.

On a content-heavy website, it often makes sense. If someone is looking for pension advice, a policy, an article, or a specific topic, a search bar can help them get there quickly.

It is like using a sat-nav to find somewhere new.

But in a workflow product, search can sometimes be a sign that the product has not guided the user well enough. If someone has to search every time they want to do a regular task, the product is making them type in the route instead of learning the journey with them.

Search also moves the effort further down the road.

The user has to type what they think the product calls something. Then they have to scan the results, guess which one looks right, and decide where to go next. The effort has not disappeared. It has just changed shape.

Search is not bad.

But it should not replace clear navigation, useful dashboards, good labels, and task-based journeys.

A sat-nav is useful when you do not know where you are going.

But you should not need it to find the shop around the corner every morning.

## Test the route

The Route Check should not end with the team's opinion.

It should create things to test.

Can users find the main task? Do they understand the labels? Where do they pause? What do they ignore? What do they click first? What takes longer than it should?

That kind of testing matters because product teams often see the product through the company's structure.

Users see it through the task they are trying to complete.

The gap between those two views is where cognitive load often lives.

If people keep missing the main action, the route is not clear enough. If they keep using search for everyday tasks, the product may not be guiding them properly. If they keep asking what a status means, the label is doing too much hidden work.

Testing helps show where the road feels smooth and where people keep hitting the same pothole.

## Reducing cognitive load is about respect

Sometimes cognitive load is just annoying.

Other times, it is dangerous.

Think about a doctor using a system to prioritise patients. Or a fleet manager trying to understand which vehicles are on the road, which ones are charging, and which ones are unavailable. Or someone using a government service, a banking product, or a legal form.

In those situations, unclear information does not just slow people down.

It can change decisions.

That is a heavy thing to put on the user.

The product should be carrying more of that weight.

To me, reducing cognitive load is not about making products childish or overly simple.

It is about respect.

It respects the fact that people arrive with limited attention, limited time, different ways of processing information, and a life already happening outside the screen.

It also recognises that users do not owe your product unlimited patience.

A good product should feel like it is working with the user, not against them.

It should understand the main journeys. It should make common tasks easier to start. It should reduce repeated choices. It should guide people through complex moments without shouting everything at once.

Cognitive load matters because everyone has a limit.

Some people reach that limit sooner. Some people reach it later. But everyone reaches it eventually.

The job of good design is not to see how much users can tolerate.

It is to help them get where they need to go without arriving exhausted.
