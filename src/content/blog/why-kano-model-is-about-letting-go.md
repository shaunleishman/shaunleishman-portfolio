The Kano Model is usually described as a way to understand customer satisfaction. It helps teams sort features into categories like basic needs, performance features, delighters, indifferent features, and reverse features. On paper, that sounds neat and structured. Almost too neat. Like one of those diagrams that looks simple until a real product team sits in a room and starts disagreeing about what everything actually means.

But to me, the Kano Model feels like something more than a prioritisation tool. I think the Kano Model is really about letting go.

Letting go of assumptions. Letting go of emotional attachment. Letting go of features that no longer serve a clear purpose. Letting go of the idea that everything we have built must still matter just because we spent time building it.

It reminds me of clearing out a house. When you clear out your home, you do not just look at what is there. You ask harder questions.

- Do I still need this?
- Does this help me?
- Does this still fit?
- Is this broken?
- Am I keeping this because it adds value, or because I feel attached to it?

That last question is usually the hardest one. It is easy to throw away something that is clearly useless. It is much harder to let go of something that used to matter. A jumper that no longer fits. A broken thing you keep meaning to fix. A novelty item you bought because it made sense for about four minutes.

Product teams collect things in a similar way. Over time, products gather features, workarounds, settings, old ideas, assumptions, stakeholder requests, and things that were built under pressure. Some of them still matter. Some of them are essential. Some of them are useful but need improving. Some once felt exciting but are now just expected. Some are barely used. Some are actively getting in the way.

The Kano Model gives teams a way to step back and ask what still deserves space.

## What the Kano Model helps us see

<!-- viz:kano-overview -->

At its simplest, the Kano Model helps teams understand how users might respond to different features.

There are must-have features. These are basic expectations. Users might not praise them when they work, but they will absolutely notice when they are missing or broken. A search bar on a website with lots of information is a good example. If users need to find something, search is not a luxury. It is expected. If it works, people move on. If it does not work, frustration appears quickly.

It is a bit like running water in a house. You do not celebrate the tap every morning for doing its job. But if it stops working, it immediately becomes the only thing you care about.

Then there are performance features. These are features where better usually means more satisfying. The more useful, accurate, fast, flexible, or effective they become, the more value they create. In everyday life, this could be a better laptop, a sharper drill, or a tool that helps you do a job with less effort. In a product, it might be reporting, filtering, automation, data accuracy, speed, or anything where improvement has a clear relationship with user satisfaction.

Then there are delighters. These are unexpected features that create a positive reaction. They might remove effort in a way the user did not expect. They might make the experience feel more thoughtful. They might add comfort, enjoyment, or surprise. In a house, delighters might be good speakers, a comfortable couch, or a small detail that makes the space feel nicer to live in.

Then there are indifferent features. These are the things users do not really care about. They exist, but they do not make much difference to satisfaction. These are the drawer items. The random purchases. The gadgets you thought would change your life but now live under a pile of instruction manuals.

Finally, there are reverse features. These are things that reduce satisfaction. They annoy users. They create friction. They make the product feel worse. In a house, these are the broken things you never fix, the clothes that no longer fit, the cables you do not understand, the drawer that jams every time you open it. A reverse feature is not neutral. It is not just taking up space. It is quietly making the experience worse.

## Letting go of emotional attachment

One of the hardest parts of product work is that teams naturally become emotionally attached to features. That is normal.

A feature is rarely just a feature to the people who built it. It can represent weeks of thinking, design, technical effort, stakeholder conversations, awkward compromises, rushed decisions, late changes, and a lot of problem-solving that nobody outside the team will ever fully see. So when someone questions whether that feature still matters, it can feel personal.

That is exactly why the Kano Model is useful. It gives the team a more neutral way to talk about value.

Instead of asking, "Do we like this feature?" we ask, "What role does this feature play for users?"

Instead of asking, "Did we work hard on this?" we ask, "Does this still create value?"

Instead of asking, "Would it feel bad to remove or change this?" we ask, "Would users miss it if it was gone?"

That shift matters. It moves the conversation away from the team's attachment and back towards the user's experience. Letting go does not mean the work did not matter. It does not mean people were wrong to build it. It simply means products change, users change, expectations change, and the value of a feature can change with them.

## Letting go of internal assumptions

The Kano Model can also help teams let go of internal assumptions.

A team might believe a feature is important because it feels important inside the organisation. It might support a business process. It might make sense to people who know the product deeply. It might be something leadership cares about. It might even be something that was important at one point in time. But internal importance is not always the same as user value.

This is where teams can fall into localised product development. By localised, I mean building around the assumptions, habits, language, and needs of the organisation. The product starts to reflect how the company thinks, rather than how users actually behave. Over time, that can become expensive. The product might need more support. More training. More explanation. More manual work. More internal knowledge just to understand it. That is usually a sign that the product is carrying too much organisational weight.

The Kano Model can help teams move towards a broader view, but only if it is used honestly. It should not be used to confirm what the team already believes. If people redefine the categories to fit their own bias, the model stops being useful. If every favourite idea becomes a delighter, every stakeholder request becomes a must-have, and every awkward problem becomes "out of scope", then Kano becomes theatre. Nice labels. Same old thinking.

The value comes when the team is willing to be challenged.

## Letting go of certainty

One of the most useful things about the Kano Model is not the final category a feature lands in. It is the disagreement that happens before the team gets there.

If one person thinks a feature is a delighter and someone else thinks it is a basic expectation, that disagreement is valuable. It might mean they understand the feature differently. It might mean they understand the Kano categories differently. It might mean they are imagining different users. It might mean the team needs more research.

That is where the real work begins.

- What does this feature actually do?
- How do users interact with it?
- What problem does it solve?
- What expectation does it meet?
- What would happen if it was removed?

These questions are useful because they slow the team down in the right place. They do not slow delivery for the sake of it. They stop the team from running confidently in different directions while pretending everyone is aligned.

That is another kind of letting go. Letting go of false certainty.

## Letting go of the idea that features stay the same forever

Another important thing about the Kano Model is that feature categories are not fixed forever.

A delighter can become a basic expectation. Something feels impressive when it first appears. Then people get used to it. Then they rely on it. Then they expect it. Eventually, if it disappears, they are not disappointed because they lost a bonus. They are frustrated because something they now consider basic has been taken away.

That is especially relevant now with AI. A feature that uses AI to turn lower-resolution data into more detailed predictions might feel like a delighter today. It can help users see patterns, fill in gaps, and understand information without needing to manually provide every data point. But as AI becomes more common, that kind of support may quickly become expected.

What feels clever today might feel basic tomorrow.

That does not mean teams should throw AI into every product like glitter into a fan. It means teams need to think carefully about the expectations they are creating. AI also brings risk. If users overtrust a prediction, the feature can become dangerous. If the system does not explain confidence, limitations, or where the data came from, then something designed to help can quickly become a source of confusion.

A helpful AI feature should not become a crutch. It should support users while still encouraging better input, better judgement, and better understanding.

Again, Kano helps us ask better questions. Not just "is this impressive?" but "what role does this play in the user's expectations?"

## Letting go through better process

For Kano to work well, the process matters. If the process is loose, biased, or too heavily influenced by loud voices, the results can become misleading.

A better approach is to give the exercise some structure.

- Define the features clearly.
- Define the Kano categories clearly.
- Let people answer privately first.
- Avoid making everything too binary.
- Use sliders, scoring, or comments to capture nuance.
- Use neutral examples before judging your own product.
- Review where the team disagrees.
- Talk through why those differences exist.
- Then, where possible, compare the team's assumptions with real user research.

That final step matters. The team's view is not the truth. It is a starting point. Sometimes the team will think something is a delighter, but users will see it as basic. Sometimes the team will think something is important, but users will not care. Sometimes the team will think something is annoying, but users will value it. Those contradictions are not failures. They are questions waiting to be answered.

## Letting go so the product can grow

For me, the Kano Model matters because it helps teams step back. It helps remove some of the emotion from product decisions without removing the care from the work. That distinction is important.

Letting go does not mean caring less. It means caring about the right thing. It means being willing to question whether a feature still deserves space. It means being honest about what users expect, what improves their experience, what delights them, what they ignore, and what frustrates them. It means accepting that a product is not there to reflect the team's attachment. It is there to create value for the people using it.

And when a product genuinely creates value, people feel it. They rely on it. They return to it. They miss it if it is gone. It becomes part of how they move forward.

That is the bigger picture. The Kano Model is not just a framework for sorting features. It is a way to clear out product clutter, challenge assumptions, and make room for what actually matters.

Sometimes the best thing a product team can do is not add more. Sometimes it is to let go.
